#### Read and convert quadratic forms (`fn-quadratic-features`, exam, 11 bank parts)

- **Representations:** **equation**, **graph**, table, words (bold: the exam uses it)
- **Unknowns:**
  - **exam** L1: given factorised form → the x-intercepts
  - new L1: given factorised form → the y-intercept

  - **exam** L1: given the vertex or vertex form → the axis of symmetry
  - **exam** L2: given general form → the axis of symmetry
  - new L2: given two points with the same y-value → the axis of symmetry
  - **exam** L2: given one root; the axis of symmetry → the other root *(reversed: the axis is given and a root is asked)*
  - new L2: given a; the axis of symmetry; c → the coefficient b *(reversed: b = −2ah)*
  - new L1: given vertex form → the vertex
  - **exam** L2: given factorised form → the vertex
  - **exam** L2: given general form → the vertex
  - new L2: given a table of values → the vertex and whether it is a maximum or minimum
  - new L1: given any form → whether the vertex is a maximum or a minimum, and that value
  - **exam** L1: given the vertex; a → h and k in a(x − h)² + k *(reversed: read the form from the point)*
  - **exam** L2: given general form → vertex form a(x − h)² + k
  - **exam** L3: given factorised form → vertex form a(x − h)² + k
  - new L2: given vertex form → general form *(reversed conversion: expand)*
  - **exam** L2: given general form → factorised form a(x − p)(x − q)
  - new L3: given vertex form with −k/a a square → factorised form
- **Angles:** x = −b/(2a); Midpoint of the x-intercepts; Read h from a(x − h)² + k; Solve f′(x) = 2ax + b = 0; Read (h, k) as the translation of y = ax²; Complete the square; x = −b/(2a), then substitute for y; Midpoint of the roots, then substitute; Set f′(x) = 0; Complete the square; Expand a(x − h)² + k and compare coefficients; Find the vertex, then write the form; Factorise ax² + bx + c; Find the roots, then write the factors.
  - *Fails:* Midpoint of the x-intercepts: no real roots (Δ < 0) means there are no roots to average; with surd roots it works but is slow.
  - *Slow:* Read h from a(x − h)² + k: completing the square just to read h takes longer than −b/(2a).
  - *Slow:* Solve f′(x) = 2ax + b = 0: needs calculus (5.3) for a one-line formula result.
  - *Slow:* Complete the square: odd b with a ≠ 1 brings fractions in early.
  - *Fails:* Midpoint of the roots, then substitute: in no-real-roots mode there is nothing to average.
  - *Slow:* Find the vertex, then write the form: quick from factorised form (h is the midpoint of the roots); from general form it is the formula route in disguise.
  - *Fails:* Factorise ax² + bx + c: in surd-roots mode no integer factor pair exists.
- **Concept probes:**
  - *interpret-parameter* (L3): In y = a(x − h)² + k, k is increased while a and h stay the same. What happens to the vertex, the axis and the roots? — The vertex moves straight up, the axis does not move, and the roots move towards the axis (for a > 0) until they merge and then vanish.
  - *always-sometimes-never* (L3): The axis of symmetry of y = ax² + bx + c passes through the y-intercept. — Sometimes: only when b = 0, because the axis is x = −b/(2a) and the y-intercept is on x = 0.
  - *true-false-reason* (L3): Two quadratics with the same roots have the same vertex. — False. They share the axis (the midpoint of the roots), but the vertex height k = −a(q − p)²/4 depends on a.
- **Misconceptions used:** 12. Uses x = b/(2a), dropping the minus sign; Writes the axis as y = h, or just the number h, instead of x = h; Reads h from a(x + 3)² as +3; From a(x + 3)² − 4 gives the vertex (3, −4) instead of (−3, −4); Finds the x-coordinate of the vertex but not the y-coordinate; Calls the vertex a minimum when a < 0; Forgets to subtract the square of half the coefficient, writing x² − 8x + 11 = (x − 4)² + 11; When a ≠ 1, does not multiply the correction term by a; Gets the sign of h wrong, writing (x + 4)² for x² − 8x + …; From (x + 1)(x − 4) gives the intercepts 1 and −4; Leaves out the leading coefficient a when writing a(x − p)(x − q); Thinks the y-intercept is pq rather than apq.

- **Unknowns:**

  - **exam** L2: given the vertex; one other point → a, then the equation (vertex form or a, b and c)
  - **exam** L2: given the vertex; one root → the other root and the equation
  - **exam** L2: given the two roots; the y-intercept or another point → a, then the equation
  - **exam** L2: given the two roots; a → b and c
  - **exam** L2: given the axis of symmetry; a → b
  - **exam** L3: given two points; the axis or a → b and c by solving two equations
  - **exam** L2: given the conditions, then the equation found from them → the range of f
  - new L3: given three points → a, b and c *(level 4 as a Paper 2 version with a GDC solver and non-integer points)*
  - new L2: given the equation with one unknown coefficient; a point on the curve → the unknown coefficient
  - new L3: given a table of values with a symmetric pair → the equation in vertex form
- **Angles:** Start from a(x − h)² + k and use a point for a; Start from a(x − p)(x − q) and use a point for a; Substitute three points into ax² + bx + c; GDC simultaneous solver or quadratic regression; Find the roots, then write the factors; Read p and q from the graph, then a from another point; Use the vertex or asymptote; x = −b/(2a); Midpoint of the x-intercepts.
  - *Slow:* Substitute three points into ax² + bx + c: three equations in a, b, c when the vertex or roots already fix two of them.
  - *Fails:* GDC simultaneous solver or quadratic regression: Paper 1 versions have no calculator.
- **Concept probes:**
  - *predict-check* (L3): A parabola has vertex (2, −8) and passes through (0, 4). Predict the sign of a before finding it, then check. — Positive: the point is above the vertex, so the curve opens upwards. Check: 4 = a(0 − 2)² − 8 gives a = 3.
  - *true-false-reason* (L4): Any three points lie on exactly one quadratic y = ax² + bx + c. — False. Three collinear points force a = 0 (a line), and two points with the same x but different y fit no function at all.
  - *interpret-parameter* (L3): Two parabolas both have roots 1 and 5. One passes through (0, 5), the other through (0, −10). What does a say about each? — y-intercept = 5a, so a = 1 for the first (opens up) and a = −2 for the second (opens down and is twice as steep).
- **Misconceptions used:** 5. Assumes a = 1 without checking against another point; Writes (x − 1) for an intercept at x = −1; Substitutes the vertex into ax² + bx + c as if it were just another point and forgets it is a turning point; Leaves out the leading coefficient a when writing a(x − p)(x − q); Writes the range in terms of x (e.g. x ≥ 2) instead of y or f(x).

#### Solve a quadratic exactly, in context or in disguise (`fn-solve-quadratic`, exam, 4 bank parts)

- **Situation:** A quadratic equation, in context or in disguise, shared with `fn-quadratic-features`, `fn-quadratic-from-conditions`, `fn-discriminant`, `fn-quadratic-inequality`, `fn-concept-quadratic-forms`.
- **Representations:** **equation**, **words**, graph (bold: the exam uses it)
- **Unknowns:**
  - **exam** L1: given a quadratic equation that factorises → both roots
  - new L1: given ax² + bx = 0 or ax² = bx → both roots, including 0
  - new L3: given a quadratic that does not factorise → the exact roots
  - **exam** L3: given a context (lengths, probabilities, a sector) → the valid root, rejecting the other
  - **exam** L3: given b²ˣ + p·bˣ + q = 0 → x, rejecting the impossible root
  - new L2: given an exponential equation with b²ˣ and bˣ → the substitution and the quadratic it gives
  - new L2: given x + c/x = b → both solutions
  - new L3: given ln x + ln(x + d) = ln c → x, rejecting the root outside the domain
  - new L3: given two roots, possibly h ± √d → a quadratic equation with integer coefficients *(reversed)*
  - new L2: given the equation with one unknown coefficient; one root → the coefficient and the other root *(reversed)*
- **Angles:** Factorise and use the zero-product rule; Quadratic formula; Complete the square and square-root; GDC polynomial solver or graph zeros; Substitute u for the repeated expression; Clear fractions or combine logs, then rearrange; Graph both sides on the GDC.
  - *Fails:* Factorise and use the zero-product rule: Δ is not a perfect square, so there are no rational factors.
  - *Slow:* Quadratic formula: taking out x is one line; the formula with c = 0 is several.
  - *Slow:* Complete the square and square-root: odd b or a ≠ 1 brings fractions in.
  - *Fails:* GDC polynomial solver or graph zeros: gives decimals, not the exact form asked for.
  - *Fails:* Graph both sides on the GDC: shows one crossing but not the exact ln form; good for seeing why a root is rejected.
- **Concept probes:**
  - *true-false-reason* (L3): If (x − 3)(x + 1) = 5, then x − 3 = 5 or x + 1 = 5. — False. The zero-product rule only works when the product is 0. Expand and rearrange: x² − 2x − 8 = 0, so x = 4 or x = −2.
  - *always-sometimes-never* (L3): Dividing both sides of an equation by x gives an equation with the same solutions. — Sometimes. It is safe only when x = 0 is not a solution; 3x² = 6x loses x = 0.
  - *predict-check* (L3): Before solving e²ˣ − eˣ − 6 = 0, predict how many solutions it has. Then solve. — One. With u = eˣ, u² − u − 6 = 0 gives u = 3 or u = −2, and eˣ = −2 is impossible, so x = ln 3 only.
- **Misconceptions used:** 6. Divides both sides of 3x² = 6x by x and loses the root x = 0; From (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0 gives x = −2 or x = −3; Forgets the ± when square-rooting, so x² = 9 gives only x = 3; Stops at u = 2 or u = 3 instead of solving e^x = 2 and e^x = 3 for x; Keeps an impossible solution such as 2^x = −1 or a negative x inside a log; Writes 4^x as 2(2^x) instead of (2^x)².

#### Discriminant conditions on a parameter (`fn-discriminant`, exam, 13 bank parts)

- **Situation:** A quadratic with a parameter in it, shared with `fn-quadratic-features`, `fn-quadratic-from-conditions`, `fn-solve-quadratic`, `fn-quadratic-inequality`, `fn-concept-quadratic-forms`.
- **Representations:** **equation**, **words**, graph (bold: the exam uses it)
- **Unknowns:**
  - **exam** L1: given a quadratic with numbers → the number of real roots, or a show-that it has none
  - new L1: given a graph of the parabola → the sign of Δ (and of a)
  - new L2: given the sign of a; the sign of Δ → which sketch fits *(pick-the-graph)*
  - new L2: given the quadratic in k; a value of k → the number of real roots or intersections *(forward direction of the type)*
  - **exam** L2: given the quadratic in k, or the line and curve → Δ as an expression in k (often a show-that)
  - **exam** L2: given the quadratic in k, or a line tangent to a curve → the value or values of k
  - **exam** L3: given the quadratic in k and a condition (two roots, none, real roots) → the interval of k
  - **exam** L3: given a quadratic in x with a parameter, inside a log or a square root → the values of k that keep it positive for all x
  - **exam** L4: given the quadratic in k → k for two distinct roots of the same sign
  - new L3: given the line and curve and the tangency condition → k and the point of contact
- **Angles:** Compute Δ = b² − 4ac and compare with 0; Compare the vertex with the x-axis; Count x-intercepts on a GDC graph; Set Δ = 0, Δ > 0 or Δ < 0 and solve for k; Complete the square and place the vertex; Match gradients for tangency; Vary the parameter on the GDC; Find the roots and sketch the parabola; Sign table of the factors; Test a point in each interval.
  - *Slow:* Compare the vertex with the x-axis: from general form the vertex has to be found first.
  - *Fails:* Count x-intercepts on a GDC graph: counting crossings is a check, not a proof, so it cannot answer a show-that.
  - *Slow:* Complete the square and place the vertex: awkward when k is in b, because h then depends on k.
  - *Fails:* Match gradients for tangency: only finds the tangent case, not the intervals either side.
  - *Fails:* Vary the parameter on the GDC: shows the change but cannot give the exact boundary.
- **Concept probes:**
  - *true-false-reason* (L3): When Δ = 0, the parabola touches the x-axis at its vertex. — True. The repeated root is x = −b/(2a), which is the axis of symmetry, so the touching point is the vertex.
  - *always-sometimes-never* (L4): If a and c have opposite signs, ax² + bx + c = 0 has two distinct real roots. — Always. Then −4ac > 0, so Δ = b² − 4ac > 0 whatever b is.
  - *interpret-parameter* (L3): The line y = x + n slides upwards as n increases, past the parabola y = x². What happens to the number of meeting points, and what does Δ = 0 mark? — None while the line is low, one at n = −1/4, then two. Equating gives x² − x − n = 0 with Δ = 1 + 4n, and Δ = 0 is the boundary where the line is a tangent.
- **Misconceptions used:** 7. Mishandles the sign of −4ac when c < 0, e.g. writes 25 − 4(1)(−6) = 1; Thinks Δ < 0 means two negative roots rather than no real roots; Uses b² + 4ac or √(b² − 4ac) as the discriminant; Reads a, b, c before rearranging to '= 0', e.g. keeps the line's terms on the other side; Solves k² − 36 < 0 as k < ±6 instead of −6 < k < 6; Uses Δ > 0 for 'real roots' and forgets the equal-roots case (Δ ≥ 0); Writes x < −2 or x > 3 as the single interval −2 < x < 3.

#### Intersect a curve and a line exactly, then use the points (`fn-curve-intersection-quadratic`, exam, 5 bank parts)

- **Situation:** A curve and a line that meet.
- **Representations:** **equation**, **graph**, words (bold: the exam uses it)
- **Unknowns:**
  - **exam** L2: given the curve and the line → the quadratic (often a show-that)
  - **exam** L2: given the curve and the line → the x-coordinates where they meet
  - **exam** L3: given the curve and the line → the coordinates of both points
  - **exam** L3: given the curve and the line → the distance between the points, or the difference of the roots, in surd form
  - **exam** L4: given the curve; a point P on it; the normal at P → the second point Q, then PQ *(level 5 when the student must also find the normal)*
  - new L2: given the curve; the two x-values where a line meets it → the equation of the line *(reversed)*
  - new L3: given the curve; y = mx + n with n unknown; one meeting x-value → n, then the other meeting point *(reversed)*
  - new L1: given a sketch of the curve and the line → how many meeting points, then confirm with algebra
- **Angles:** Set f(x) = g(x) and solve; Sketch both graphs to count and locate crossings; GDC intersect; Zeros of f(x) − g(x); Factorise and use the zero-product rule; Quadratic formula; Complete the square and square-root; Combine and rearrange into a polynomial; Sketch or graph both sides to check; m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁).
  - *Fails:* GDC intersect: decimals cannot give the surd form asked for.
  - *Fails:* Factorise and use the zero-product rule: surd roots do not factor.
  - *Slow:* Quadratic formula: one root (P) is already known, so factorising out (x − x_P) is faster.
- **Concept probes:**
  - *true-false-reason* (L3): A straight line can meet a parabola y = ax² + bx + c at three points. — False. Equating gives a quadratic equation, which has at most two real roots.
  - *predict-check* (L3): Predict from a sketch how many times y = x² − 4 meets y = 2x − 6. Then check algebraically. — None: x² − 2x + 2 = 0 has Δ = 4 − 8 = −4 < 0, so the line passes below the parabola.
  - *interpret-parameter* (L4): The chord between the meeting points has length |x₂ − x₁|·√(1 + m²). Why does the line's gradient m appear? — The horizontal gap is |x₂ − x₁| and the rise is m times that, so by Pythagoras the length is the gap times √(1 + m²).
- **Misconceptions used:** 6. Gives only the x-coordinate when coordinates are asked for; Finds one intersection and misses others hidden outside the GDC window; Rounds x early and substitutes, giving an inaccurate y-coordinate; From (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0 gives x = −2 or x = −3; Keeps a solution that makes a log argument negative or a denominator zero; Multiplies only one side by the denominator when clearing fractions.

#### Solve a quadratic inequality (`fn-quadratic-inequality`, exam, 2 bank parts)

- **Situation:** A quadratic compared with a number, shared with `fn-quadratic-features`, `fn-quadratic-from-conditions`, `fn-solve-quadratic`, `fn-discriminant`, `fn-concept-quadratic-forms`.
- **Representations:** **equation**, **words**, graph (bold: the exam uses it)
- **Unknowns:**
  - **exam** L2: given f(x) compared with 0 → the solution set
  - **exam** L3: given f(x) compared with d ≠ 0 → the solution set, after rearranging
  - **exam** L4: given a context with an inequality → the largest (or smallest) whole number allowed
  - new L1: given x² compared with n² → the solution set
  - new L2: given an inequality with a < 0 → the solution set
  - new L3: given an inequality whose quadratic has Δ < 0 → all x, or no x
  - new L3: given a solution set such as −2 < x < 3 → a quadratic inequality with that solution *(reversed)*
- **Angles:** Find the roots and sketch the parabola; Sign table of the factors; Test a point in each interval; Graph on the GDC and read the interval.
  - *Fails:* Sign table of the factors: there are no factors to tabulate.
  - *Fails:* Graph on the GDC and read the interval: Paper 1 needs exact roots, not decimal zeros.
- **Concept probes:**
  - *always-sometimes-never* (L3): If x² > 9, then x > 3. — Sometimes. x = 4 fits, but x = −4 also has x² > 9 and is not greater than 3. The full answer is x < −3 or x > 3.
  - *interpret-parameter* (L3): For a > 0, why does the solution of ax² + bx + c > 0 come in two pieces, but ax² + bx + c < 0 in one? — An upward parabola is below the axis only between its roots and above it on both sides.
  - *predict-check* (L3): Predict the solution of (x − 1)² ≥ 0 without expanding. Then predict (x − 1)² < 0. — All real x; then no x at all. A square is never negative.
- **Misconceptions used:** 3. Solves x² > 9 as x > ±3, or as x > 3 only; Writes x < −2 or x > 3 as the single interval −2 < x < 3; Divides by a negative leading coefficient without reversing the inequality.

#### Expand brackets (`fn-pre-expand`, prior-learning, level 0–0)

*Gap:* Prior learning the IB assumes. Converting between quadratic forms fails here first.

- **Situation:** Brackets to expand.
- **Supports:** `complete-square`, `find-equation`, `factorised-form`, `discriminant-parameter`
- **Representations:** equation, graph (bold: the exam uses it)
- **Unknowns:**
  - new L0: given k·x(x + r) → the expansion
  - new L0: given (x + r)(x + s) → the expansion
  - new L0: given (x + r)² → the expansion
  - new L0: given k(x + r)(x + s) → the expansion
  - new L0: given k(x + r)² + d → the expansion
  - new L0: given (x + r)² = x² + bx + c with r unknown and b given → r and c *(reversed)*
- **Angles:** Multiply every term by every term; Area grid; Use (x + r)² = x² + 2rx + r²; Check with a number.
  - *Slow:* Area grid: the factor in front and the constant are extra steps outside the grid.
  - *Fails:* Check with a number: checks an answer but does not find one.
- **Concept probes:**
  - *true-false-reason* (L3): (x + 4)² = x² + 16. — False. (x + 4)(x + 4) has two middle products 4x + 4x, so it is x² + 8x + 16; try x = 1: 25, not 17.
- **Misconceptions used:** 4. Squares each term: (x + r)² = x² + r²; Multiplies the factor in front into only the first bracket term; Gets the sign of a product wrong: (x + 3)(x − 5) = x² + 2x − 15; In k(x + r)² + d, multiplies d by k as well.

#### Factorise expressions (`fn-pre-factorise`, prior-learning, level 0–0)

*Gap:* Prior learning the IB assumes. Solving, finding roots and simplifying rational functions all need it.

- **Situation:** An expression with known factors.
- **Supports:** `factorised-form`, `solve-equation`, `inequality`, `hidden-quadratic`
- **Representations:** equation, graph (bold: the exam uses it)
- **Unknowns:**
  - new L0: given kx² + mx → the factorised form
  - new L0: given x² + bx + c → the factorised form
  - new L0: given p²x² − q² → the factorised form
  - new L0: given ax² + bx + c with a > 1 → the factorised form
  - new L0: given a product and a sum → the two numbers
  - new L0: given (x + r)(x + □) = x² + bx + c → the missing number *(reversed)*
- **Angles:** Two numbers with product ac and sum b; Split the middle term and group; Recognise a pattern; Solve first, then write the factors; Expand to check.
  - *Slow:* Split the middle term and group: grouping is unnecessary when a = 1.
  - *Slow:* Solve first, then write the factors: the formula is long for an easy factorisation.
- **Concept probes:**
  - *always-sometimes-never* (L3): x² + k factorises over the integers. — Sometimes: only when k is the negative of a square (x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x + 3)). x² + 9 does not factorise.
- **Misconceptions used:** 4. Swaps the signs: x² − x − 6 = (x − 2)(x + 3); Factorises a sum of squares: x² + 9 = (x + 3)(x − 3); Divides only the first term by the common factor: 6x² − 9x = 3x(2x − 9); Ignores the leading coefficient: 2x² + 7x + 3 = (x + 1)(x + 3).

#### Simplify surds (`fn-pre-surds`, prior-learning, level 0–0)

*Gap:* Prior learning the IB assumes. Exact roots and chord lengths are marked in simplest surd form.

- **Situation:** Surds built from a square factor.
- **Supports:** `solve-equation`, `complete-square`
- **Representations:** equation (bold: the exam uses it)
- **Unknowns:**
  - new L0: given √n → a√b
  - new L0: given a sum of two unsimplified surds → one simplified surd
  - new L0: given √m × √n → a√b
  - new L0: given (m ± √n)/d → its simplest form
  - new L0: given a√b → √n *(reversed)*
- **Angles:** Take out the largest square factor; Prime factorise and pair; Square to check.
  - *Slow:* Take out the largest square factor: taking a smaller square first (√48 = 2√12) needs a second pass.
- **Concept probes:**
  - *true-false-reason* (L3): √(9 + 16) = √9 + √16. — False: √25 = 5, but 3 + 4 = 7. Roots split over products, not sums.
- **Misconceptions used:** 3. Splits a root over a sum: √(a + b) = √a + √b; Divides only one term of the numerator: (4 + √12)/2 = 4 + √3; Stops before the surd is fully simplified, e.g. √48 = 2√12.

#### Solve linear inequalities (`fn-pre-linear-inequalities`, prior-learning, level 0–0)

*Gap:* Prior learning the IB assumes. Quadratic inequalities and discriminant conditions end in one.

- **Situation:** A linear inequality with a known boundary.
- **Supports:** `inequality`, `discriminant-parameter`
- **Representations:** equation, graph, words (bold: the exam uses it)
- **Unknowns:**
  - new L0: given ax + b < c → the solution
  - new L0: given c₁ < ax + b ≤ c₂ → the solution
  - new L0: given a number line → the inequality *(reversed)*
  - new L0: given an inequality → how many integers satisfy it
- **Angles:** Undo each operation on both sides; Collect x on the side where its coefficient is positive; Test a value.
  - *Slow:* Collect x on the side where its coefficient is positive: with three parts, moving terms is clumsier than dividing and reversing.
- **Concept probes:**
  - *true-false-reason* (L3): From −2x > 6 you get x > −3. — False: dividing by −2 reverses the sign, so x < −3. Test x = 0: −2(0) > 6 is false, and 0 > −3.
- **Misconceptions used:** 3. Keeps the sign when dividing by a negative; Confuses open and closed circles, so < and ≤ swap; In a two-sided inequality, applies the step to the middle part only.
